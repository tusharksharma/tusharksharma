# Creative direction, intake and songwriting

These are specialist working methods, not provider guarantees. Select what serves the brief; do not force a genre, vocal style or questionnaire onto every song.

## Intake that leads to decisions

Start with a compact interview, for example:

1. Do you have a similar song or link? Which part do you like: the beat, instruments, energy, vocal delivery or hook? No reference is fine.
2. How many seconds or minutes should the finished music be? Does it need a clean ending or a seamless loop?
3. What is it for: recipe background, intro, outro, montage, ad or a standalone song? Will someone speak over it, and when?
4. How should it feel? Any required words, language, or preference for instrumental versus singing? I can recommend those choices.

Ask only unresolved follow-ups that change the music: reveal timestamps, tempo/genre dislikes, language and pronunciation, singer character, fixed slogan, explicit-content boundary or loop length. For execution also establish UI/API route, available plan/model and cap. Do not ask for a subscription plan just to write a prompt. Do not ask for key signatures or chord progressions unless the user wants that control.

Record exact target length, opening deadline, speech intervals, important visual hits and ending behavior. Distinguish whole track from the excerpt used in an edit. An intro, background bed and outro can share a motif without sharing arrangement or loudness. If essential timing is unknown, ask; if the user says to choose, state a reasonable provisional choice.

## Decide the role of the human voice

| Use | Starting recommendation | Why / adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recipe explanation or host speech | Instrumental | Lyrics compete for language attention; simplify lead instruments too. |
| Short brand mnemonic without narration | Brief sung or chanted hook | The words can carry recall; avoid squeezing a verse into a sting. |
| Full standalone song | Vocals when the story matters | Build a coherent lyrical arc and room for breath. |
| Food montage without speech | Instrumental or vocal hook | Decide whether rhythm/texture or lyrical identity is doing the work. |
| Music around narration | Hybrid | Sing before/after speech, with an explicitly instrumental middle. |
| Wordless atmosphere | Hums/choir only if useful | This still contains vocals and can mask speech; never call it instrumental. |
| Closing card of five seconds or less | Short resolved phrase | Avoid a new lyrical thought that cannot finish. |

These are recommendations. An explicit vocal request wins; propose placement and a sparse arrangement that can work. If the user asks the specialist to decide, choose and explain without another approval round. A request for "no vocals" excludes humming, chants, choir, vocal chops, spoken words and ad-libs as well as lyrics.

## Translate a reference into a new composition

Create a private reference card: source and time range; inspected versus user-described evidence; liked attributes; attributes to change; broad genre/era, pulse, groove/swing, bass movement, instrument roles, density, emotional arc and production space. Do not pretend a webpage description establishes an exact BPM or key. Use measured values only when actually analyzed, otherwise choose a target and label it as a creative choice.

Extract the underlying appeal: e.g. a bouncing offbeat bass and clipped rhythm guitar rather than a recognizable riff. A raspy, playful original lead is a timbral direction; impersonating an identifiable singer is not needed. Compose a fresh hook, melody and lyrics. Do not reproduce a distinctive lyric with a few words replaced. Reference titles belong in research notes, never in the provider prompt, styles, chunk names or uploaded metadata.

## Arrange for the available time

Choose one identity: a central motif or groove plus a small supporting palette. Define what bass, drums, rhythm instrument and lead each contribute. Avoid stacking unrelated genre words with no hierarchy. Energy can rise by adding percussion or widening harmony without a tempo change. Give the opening its intended job immediately; do not spend half a short cue warming up.

For approximate phrase planning in 4/4, seconds per bar = 240 / BPM. At 120 BPM, four bars take eight seconds. Fit complete phrases and the final decay inside the requested length; do not assume eight bars equals eight seconds. Visual hits may require a pickup, shorter phrase or separate ending. Timestamp cues are an arrangement plan, not evidence of achieved synchronization.

Under narration, favor stable dynamics, restrained cymbals, sparse melodic motion and space for consonants. Save fills and lead hooks for speech gaps. A prompt requesting headroom cannot replace mixing or ducking later. A cooking bed should not invent sizzling/chopping sounds that could be mistaken for real footage evidence.

For a loop, plan matching pulse, harmony and texture across the boundary. Avoid a final crash or decaying tail unless the loop design accommodates it. Verify the repeated audio seam later. For a resolved finish, stop new lyric content early enough for a cadence and decay; never solve excess length by clipping a word.

## Write as a lyricist

Choose a single thought and point of view before rhyming. For a jingle, use a short memorable phrase, a specific brand idea and room for the brand name. For a full song, let verses add concrete details and the chorus repeat the emotional thesis; a bridge should offer a changed perspective rather than padding.

Draft the hook first, then read all lines aloud at the intended pulse. Put stressed syllables on plausible strong beats. Match repeated phrases in syllable shape, allow breaths, and prefer natural wording to forced end rhyme. Leave vowels that can sustain at phrase endings. Avoid dense multisyllabic copy in a fast short hook. There is no universal safe word count: perform a rough spoken/sung timing pass and simplify when the line cannot fit comfortably.

Separate the lyric sheet from instrumentation notes. Make intentional repeats explicit; do not rely on "repeat chorus" when exact words matter. Keep diction, vocalist character, register and harmonies in direction fields. Verify brand/food-name pronunciation with the user when uncertain; do not assume TTS dictionaries, SSML or a saved speaking voice work in Music. Preserve the intended cultural pronunciation. For multilingual writing, confirm language/script, natural idiom and code-switch boundaries; mark uncertain native-language QA honestly.

Family-friendly is the existing channel starting point. Do not introduce shaming about body size, kids, food shortcuts or convenience ingredients. Do not invent protein numbers, recipe claims or personal family stories for a rhyme.

## Listening review and learning

Review the generated audio, when available, for: exact beginning/end and silence, unwanted voices, lyric fidelity and diction, musical landing, artifacts/clipping, rhythmic feel, motif identity and speech masking in context. Try phone/small-speaker and mono playback when the track will be used on social video. Metadata checks cannot prove musical quality. If audio inspection is unavailable, deliver a checklist and clearly mark the track unreviewed.

Use specific feedback: timestamp, symptom, likely cause, smallest next change. "Too busy under the host at 0:12" suggests removing lead/fills in that interval before changing the genre. "Words rushed" suggests fewer syllables or more phrase time before a new singer. Keep the accepted take as a baseline. Save a private record of prompt/version, settings, lyrics, observed duration, reference rights, costs, file identifier, review and accepted changes. A preference history builds expertise; it does not prove retention or copyright clearance.
