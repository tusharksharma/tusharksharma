**Make your audience specific enough that every series feels connected.**

Your primary audience should be **parents who care about eating well and staying active, but have limited time and different preferences around the dinner table**.

Their recurring needs give you the editorial direction:

- “Help me get dinner done.”
- “Show me something useful I can remember.”
- “Tell me whether this product or trend is worth trying.”
- “Make family life feel relatable.”
- “Show me how you fit this into an actual working day.”

Protein enthusiasts, Creami owners, and curious home cooks can enter through particular series. The broader account should help them understand the person and philosophy behind those posts.

A proposed bio:

> High-protein food. Real family life.  
> One cook, two plates + shortcuts worth repeating.  
> Recipes & weekly plans ↓

Use three pinned posts to establish the account:

1. **Your origin story:** what led you to build The Split Plate, using your actual experience.
2. **The method demonstrated:** one appealing meal becoming adult and kid plates.
3. **The starting point:** three easy dinners and where to find their recipes.

Your family-dinner posts should visibly demonstrate the split. Coffee, product reviews, workouts, and experiments can stand on their own while retaining your practical, personal perspective.

**Give every post one primary job.**

Your calendar already distinguishes reach, growth, community, and monetization. Keep that structure, but define success differently for each.

| Job | What the viewer gets | Appropriate next action | Main measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reach** | A useful tip, surprising result, or compelling demonstration | Watch, share, explore | Non-follower reach, retention, shares |
| **Growth** | A recognizable series worth returning for | Visit profile, follow | Follows attributed to posts; profile conversion |
| **Community** | A relatable experience or worthwhile conversation | Reply, participate, return | Substantive comments, repeat participation |
| **Monetization** | Evidence that helps with a purchase decision | View details, click, buy where relevant | Qualified clicks, conversions, revenue |

A product appearance does not automatically make a recipe a monetization post. A review should answer a purchasing question: how you use it, what it does well, what disappoints you, and who would find it worthwhile.

Your existing mix targets approximately 25% reach, 25% growth, 30% community, and 20% monetization. Treat these as planning guides. I would initially test **three strong product/use-case posts per week**, then increase only if audience response and commercial results justify it. **Organize your content around six connected pillars.**

| Pillar | Existing series and formats | Editorial direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Meals that fit real life** | Split Plate dinners, Quick Lunches, Snack Boxes, Sauce Series | Show the specific problem solved: limited time, leftovers, different preferences, limited cleanup. |
| **Useful food knowledge** | Seven-second facts, preparation tips, ingredient comparisons | Teach one thing viewers can apply immediately. |
| **Experiments and discovery** | Viral Recipes Proteinized, original-recipe experiments, Wildcard Lab | Show the question, test, result, and honest verdict. |
| **Desserts and enjoyable routines** | CrumblCreamiCut, Proffee | Combine appealing food with recognizable recurring formats. |
| **The person behind the system** | Yapping, What I Eat, workouts, comedy, household routines | Explain your choices and show how they work in practice. |
| **Products that earn their place** | Genuine-use reviews, restocks, equipment demonstrations | Give specific purchase guidance grounded in your own use. |

For **family dinners**, the central story is the decision that keeps the cook shared. Show where the sauce, portion, presentation, or finishing step changes. Keep adult macro information clearly attached to the adult serving.

For **food knowledge**, expand beyond nutrition numbers. Your preparation-tip results warrant tests around cutting, assembling, portioning, reducing mess, using leftovers, and reading product labels. A useful lesson can be the whole post.

For **experiments**, preserve the distinction between your two existing series:

- **Viral Recipes Proteinized:** adapt an existing trend and judge whether the change improved it.
- **Can I Make a Recipe Go Viral?:** develop your own idea and document the attempt.

Wildcard Lab can supply original concepts for testing. It does not need another compulsory publishing slot. Failed tests can become honest verdict videos; only recipes you would recommend should become finished recipe packages.

For **CrumblCreamiCut**, keep your recognizable weekly sequence: teaser, three flavors, ranking. Give each installment different value. Reserve first reactions and comparative judgments for the ranking so it adds something beyond a recap.

For **personal content**, lean into specific choices: why you use grocery delivery, what you keep frozen, what you refuse to compromise on, how training fits into your week. Describe what works in your household without turning it into universal parenting or nutrition advice.

**Make the opening communicate the value before introducing the series.**

A viewer should understand what is happening from the first shot and first sentence. Put episode numbers in a small label or caption.

Use different structures for different purposes:

| Format | Suggested starting length to test | Structure |
|---|---:|---|
| Practical tip | 8–20 seconds | Result/problem → demonstration → finished benefit |
| Quick meal | 15–30 seconds | Finished food → key actions → serving/use case |
| Full recipe | 30–60 seconds, longer when useful | Payoff → essential process → split/result → next action |
| Personal opinion | 25–60 seconds | Specific belief → real example → tradeoff → question |
| Experiment | 30–75 seconds | Question → attempt → result → honest verdict |
| Product review | 20–45 seconds | Purchase question → demonstration → drawback → verdict |

These are test ranges, not mandatory limits.

For a family dinner, a possible opening is:

> “Same beef. My burger, their sliders, tomorrow’s lunch.”

For an experiment:

> “This looked better online. Here’s what happened when I made it.”

For useful knowledge:

> “Here’s the cut that makes this easier to serve.”

Use only claims and experiences that match the footage. Keep the food visible, captions readable, and important text away from interface controls. Your native sound and silent-performance formats should remain part of the mix.

**Build the production process around capturing several distinct stories during one activity.**

Before cooking or testing, write a short brief containing:

- Audience problem.
- Primary job: reach, growth, community, or monetization.
- Opening shot and sentence.
- Required evidence or demonstration.
- Final payoff.
- One next action.
- Footage reserved for later edits.

During a dinner shoot, capture the finished meal, essential technique, adult serving, kid variation where relevant, ingredient/product use, and your immediate reaction. Reserve different shots for the teaser, main, and follow-ups, consistent with your existing inventory rules.

A practical weekly workflow:

| Session | Work |
|---|---|
| **Planning: 30–45 minutes** | Review results, choose topics, check ready backlog, identify missing footage. |
| **Cooking/testing sessions** | Film meals and experiments you intend to make; separate extra production time from ordinary cooking. |
| **Pickup session: 30–45 minutes** | Record product labels, comparisons, fact demonstrations, and personal introductions. |
| **Editing batches** | Group similar formats so captions, sound, and exports are easier to handle. |
| **Publishing/community blocks** | Check final assets and links; respond thoughtfully to substantive comments. |
| **Weekly review: 30 minutes** | Decide what to repeat, change, carry forward, or stop. |

Track actual hours for two weeks. Twenty-eight posts can consume considerable time even with an established workflow; reduce flexible output if it crowds out testing quality or audience interaction.

Continue separating **recipe coverage** from **exact edit status**. A recipe being posted does not mean its adult cut, teaser, or standalone component edit was posted.

**Connect attention to a useful next step.**

The intended path should be:

**Useful post → relevant profile promise → matching recipe or guide → reason to return.**

Create or designate one clear starting resource:

> **Five dinners, one grocery list, two plates.**

Give new visitors something they can use immediately. From there, invite them to a weekly email containing one dinner, one quick lunch or shortcut, one useful lesson, and a link to the weekly plan.

Match the destination to the post:

| Post | Useful destination |
|---|---|
| Family dinner | That exact recipe |
| Meal-planning explanation | Weekly plan and grocery list |
| Creami comparison | Relevant recipe collection |
| Product review | Detailed use cases and alternatives |
| Brand introduction | The starting guide |

Choose one call to action per post. “Save this,” “which would you choose?” and “get the full recipe” serve different purposes.

Keep “comment recipe” prompts limited to situations where a reliable reply process exists and the viewer receives the promised help promptly.

**Develop commercial credibility through evidence of actual use.**

Your strongest potential partner story is that you demonstrate products working within repeatable household routines.

Start by documenting three review types:

1. **Repeat purchase:** what you keep buying and why.
2. **Tradeoff:** where a product is useful and where you prefer something else.
3. **Demonstration:** a product solving a specific cooking problem.

Distinguish independent purchases, gifted items, paid work, and affiliate relationships clearly.

After several weeks, build a compact case study with audience context, representative posts, median performance, relevant comments, and measured clicks or conversions where available. Keep organic editorial performance separate from paid results.

I would postpone a substantial paid-growth program until the profile-to-website path works and several formats repeatedly attract the audience you want.

**Measure whether each series fulfills its purpose.**

Add or maintain these fields in your existing tracker:

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Series and primary job | Makes comparisons meaningful |
| Opening type and runtime | Helps identify packaging patterns |
| Published date and platform | Supports equal-age comparisons |
| Views/reach at seven days | Establishes a consistent review window |
| Watch time and retention, where available | Shows where attention is lost |
| Shares, saves, substantive comments | Captures usefulness and response |
| Post-attributed follows, where available | Measures audience growth |

Compare posts **within the same platform, format, and age window**. Keep video and carousel results separate. A zero duration in the export needs format classification; it should not be treated as evidence of an exceptionally short video.

Use medians to reduce the influence of the watermelon breakout. Keep missing metrics blank, and verify unusual engagement counts against native analytics before making strategic decisions.

Run one focused experiment at a time:

| Test | Change | Hold reasonably consistent |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Finished result versus problem-first | Series, approximate length, production quality |
| Recipe detail | Concise demonstration versus fuller explanation | Similar meal complexity |
| Personal presence | Direct introduction versus food-led opening | Topic and core message |
| Next action | Follow for the series versus save the recipe | Content purpose and format |

Publish several examples of each approach before deciding. These are directional comparisons; different topics and audiences prevent them from being controlled experiments.
